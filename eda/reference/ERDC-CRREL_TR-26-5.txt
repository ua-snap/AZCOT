Abstract 
The US military develops and updates environmental parameters specified 
for the sustainment of operations throughout the world. These 
requirements are generally based on environmental data providing a 
baseline of temperature, wind, and precipitation expectations for each 
location. Observational data for Arctic regions is limited because of the 
remote and sparsely occupied geographical conditions. To address the 
need for updating these requirements, a 30-year analysis of meteorological 
conditions was conducted using a European Centre for Medium-Range 
Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) global reanalysis dataset over the Arctic and 
Subarctic region, defined by latitude 60°–90° North for this project. Raw 
hourly datasets were acquired, and the minimum temperatures, maximum 
wind speeds, maximum snow depths, and averages were determined over 
the period 1991–2020 between the months of October and March for each 
parameter. These were then visualized with geospatial analysis, producing 
a variety of maps designed to assist with the classification of parameters in 
Arctic zones of operation across a range of temporal resolutions. Finally, a 
review of operational limits for military equipment was conducted to 
match northern zones of operation with suitable capabilities dependent on 
environmental conditions.

1 Introduction 
The National Strategy for the Arctic Region (DOS 2022) highlights the 
overarching strategic policies for US interests in the region. The DoD has 
also recently released an Arctic strategy (DoD 2024), as well as the 
individual Services with the Air Force (DAF 2020), Army (HQDA and DAF 
2021), Navy (DON 2019), and other DoD entities requiring an increased 
presence across the Arctic for safeguarding the region. Mission planners 
must be assured the warfighter has full personnel support and operational 
facilities which can be quickly deployed and robust enough to maintain life 
sustainment in very harsh Arctic conditions, and are quickly recoverable 
with organic assets not requiring significant logistical support from lateral 
units. DoD planners have identified stringent environmental parameters 
(i.e., for extended operations down to −65°F) to be attained by 
infrastructure and equipment.1 This is because of the difficult operating 
conditions of extreme cold regions where some equipment and materials 
will not function at certain environmental thresholds, and military 
operations have hard-stop operating specifications. Applying rigid 
requirements for environmental parameters throughout the Arctic and 
Subarctic is impractical in regions where those environmental conditions 
rarely occur. This project provides a method to apply appropriate 
environmental conditions using data analysis for specific areas within the 
Arctic and Subarctic regions from latitudes between 60° and 90° North. 
1.1 Background 
The Arctic and Subarctic are vast (Figure 1) and cover a variety of terrain 
and atmospheric conditions. The Army Technical Manual (TM) (DA 2017) 
introduces the general principles and technical guidance for performing 
field maintenance, operation, and repair of equipment under cold weather 
conditions specifically in below freezing temperatures. Also, the Army 
Regulation (AR) 70-38 (HQDA 2020) provides Cold and Severe Cold 
Climatic Categories. These areas include northern North America, 
Greenland, northern Asia, and Tibet. The categories for cold conditions in 
AR 70-38, are divided into four elements (basic cold, cold, severe cold, and 
extreme cold; Table 1) and represent only the upper and lower limits of the 
daily temperature cycles. The daily extremes represent the highest and

1.2 Objective 
The objective of this effort is to further identify and refine the prevailing 
zonal characterization in the circumpolar north identified in TM 4-33.31 
and AR 70-38. This zonal characterization includes three environmental 
parameters (air temperature extremes, snow depth, and wind speed limits) 
and mapped these against material and equipment exposure thresholds. 
Operational zone characterization in the Arctic and Subarctic regions 
involves defining environmental limits to identify the contingency, 
logistics and resources needed, and to develop site-focused requirements 
for military operations in the Arctic and Subarctic regions. This provides 
mission-critical information that helps operations managers make risk-
informed decisions based on the analysis of recent weather data. 
1.3 Approach 
To accomplish the intended project objectives, we used a 30-year span of 
meteorological data (described in the methodology below), validated and 
verified by observational point data. After the datasets are processed, we 
applied various metric calculations and generated maps for each 
parameter (Figure 3).

The datasets used are described in Section . The processes or methodology 
for generating maps to visualize the environmental conditions for Arctic 
and Subarctic zones of operation is described in Section 2.3 as well as in 
Barrett et al (2025). Because cold environments are particularly difficult 
for military operations (soldiers, materials, equipment, etc.), this project 
also aimed to compile operational thresholding tables to map appropriate 
materiel to each zone of operation.

2 Methodology 
2.1 Area of Study 
The study region includes all areas between latitudes of 60°N and 90°N, 
rather than adhering to the established Arctic boundary at 66.5°N. 
Stakeholders requested this broader region to include both Arctic and 
Subarctic zones for analysis of a larger region of land with high mission 
activity, especially in the parts of Alaska and Canada between 60°N and 
66.5°N latitude. As a result, the analyses and resulting statistics are not 
representative of the Arctic region alone or of any single nation, but rather 
the environmental patterns within the 60°N to 90°N band. This 
distinction is important for interpreting results and understanding the 
scope and applicability of the data. 
2.2 Data 
There are many datasets that can be used to determine atmospheric and 
terrain characterization parameters within the Arctic and near-Arctic. 
While point observations often provide the most accurate data at specific 
locations, data availability in the Arctic is typically both sparse and 
inconsistent because of extreme weather conditions, low population, 
financial considerations, and other factors. Military stakeholders, 
including mission planners at DoD installations, often have requirements 
to deploy in areas where there have been minimal meteorological 
observations. In these cases, the use of a numerical weather prediction 
(NWP) model analysis dataset with initialization values may be used. 
Weather analysis datasets are an established and accepted procedure to 
project the state of the atmosphere from a finite set of imperfect, 
irregularly distributed observations onto a regular grid, or to represent the 
atmospheric state by the amplitude of standard mathematical functions, 
allowing for easy diagnostic study (AMS 2024a). An analysis may be 
considered as a space-time interpolation system. Essential ingredients of 
an analysis are a background field, usually a short-range forecast 
(although persistence may be an option), a forecast model, knowledge of 
the relative error of the many different observational platforms employed 
as well as knowledge of the error in the background field, and the spatial 
covariance of the various errors.

A specialized analysis known as reanalysis may provide an advantage over 
a standard analysis. Meteorological studies made with results from 
standard analyses may introduce inconsistences from frequent changes in 
the NWP model configuration used to generate forecasts from previous 
analysis times. These changes (including changes in resolution and 
orography) lead to discontinuities in a time series of real-time analyses 
(AMS 2024b). A reanalysis produces complete, global gridded data that 
are as temporally homogeneous as possible. In the case of a reanalysis, the 
initial forecast (also known as the background field) is made by an NWP 
model that does not change over the entire period of the reanalysis. 
Reanalysis data may include many derived fields (heating, soil moisture 
over land, etc.) for which direct observations are nearly absent. Reanalysis 
combines model data with observations from across the world into a 
globally complete and consistent dataset using atmospheric physics. A 
reanalysis may produce data that goes several decades back, providing an 
accurate description of existing past atmospheric conditions. 
2.2.1 European Center for Medium-Range Weather Forecasting (ECMWF) 
Reanalysis v5 (ERA5) as Baseline Gridded Dataset 
According to AR 115-10 AFI 15-157 Interservice Publication (IP) Section 3-
2, the United States Air Force (USAF) is statutorily responsible for 
meteorological operations in support of the Army Headquarters, 
Department of the Army (HQDA). This includes access to global surface 
and upper air meteorological observations, surface radar data, satellite 
imagery from geostationary, and polar orbiting sensors, as well as gridded 
datasets from NWP models. USAF has established multiple groups to 
assist with the variety of needs required for military applications. For this 
research, we relied on the expertise of the 14th Weather Squadron (14WS), 
located in Asheville, North Carolina. The 14WS is generally responsible for 
exploiting authoritative historical meteorological data to optimize military 
operations. After multiple conversations and the submission of a Support 
Analysis Request (SAR) (USAF 2017), 14WS recommended the usage of a 
reanalysis from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather 
Forecasts (ECMWF) known as ECMWF Reanalysis v5 (ERA5) (Hersbach 
et al 2020). 
ERA5 is a reanalysis dataset providing a consistent view of the evolution of 
meteorological and other parameters over several decades (ECMWF 
2018). The reanalysis combines model data with observations from across 
the world into a globally complete and consistent dataset. ERA5 provides 
@@@ PAGE 18 @@@
ERDC/CRREL TR-26-5 8 
 
hourly estimates for a large number of atmospheric and land surface 
parameters. A major strength of ERA5 is the much higher temporal and 
spatial resolution than those of previous global reanalyses. 
The ERA5 dataset, as with any simulation, provides estimates which have 
some degree of uncertainty. Numerical models can only provide a 
representation of the real physical processes governing different 
components of the Earth System. In general, the uncertainty of model 
estimates grows larger as we go back in time, because the number of 
observations available to create a good quality atmospheric forcing is lower. 
The temporal resolution is hourly while the gridded spatial resolution is 
0.25° (approximately 31 km). Thus, the ERA5 dataset provides an ideal, 
detailed snapshot of a variety of meteorological parameters. 
2.2.2 Data Selection and Transformation 
The parameters and threshold analyses was performed for the 60°N–90°N 
latitude regions over a thirty year period. Data temporal range is based on 
the recognized international standard. The World Meteorological 
Organization (WMO) has established that thirty years is the standard time 
period required for calculating averages. Furthermore, WMO states that 
the most recent thirty-year period finishing in a year ending with 0 is the 
standard that should be utilized for data comparisons (WMO 2017). For 
this work, the years from 1991 to 2020 were chosen as the most recent 
available. This also serves to match other similar datasets produced by 
other meteorological agencies, such as the NOAA National Centers for 
Environmental Information, allowing additional dataset comparisons. It is 
expected that the products produced by this project would be updated on a 
decadal basis to continue to match future long term meteorological 
datasets. As the stakeholders for the project were primarily concerned with 
data and extremes occurring during winter months, only the months 
between October to March were considered for analysis. 
The data subset used contained latitudes between 60°N to 90°N latitude to 
cover all Arctic regions plus portions of Subarctic regions. The data was 
obtained from the ECMWF Climate Data Store (also known as Copernicus) 
(ECMWF n.d.). The site provides free access to a variety of ECMWF 
maintained datasets; it allows both manual downloads and a Python API 
for more advanced data acquisition. The ERA5 data was initially obtained 
from ECMWF in gridded binary (GRIB) format. GRIB is a format based on 
standards established by the WMO and is typically used by operational 
@@@ PAGE 19 @@@
ERDC/CRREL TR-26-5 9 
 
centers that produce NWP products. All parameters were downloaded in a 
single hourly GRIB formatted file. Parameters selected include 2 m height 
air temperature, skin (land surface) temperature, 10 m wind components, 
snow water equivalent (SWE), and level 1 soil temperature. Additionally, 
at the request of the stakeholders, snow depth was obtained from a 
derivative ECMWF dataset known as ERA5-Land (ECMWF 2025c). This 
dataset provides higher spatial resolution at 0.1° (approximately 9 km) at 
the expense of exclusion of data over oceanic and other water surface 
areas. Details about the collected variables are below: 
• Skin temperature (SKT)—the temperature at the land surface, in Kelvin 
• 2 m temperature (2T)—the temperature at a height of 2 m, in Kelvin 
• Soil temperature, Level 1 (STL1)—the average temperature 0–7 cm 
below the surface, in Kelvin 
• Wind velocity, U-Component (U)—the easterly wind velocity at a 
height of 10 m, in meters per second 
• Wind velocity, V-Component (V)—the northerly wind velocity at a 
height of 10 m, in meters per second 
• Snow depth (SD)—the depth of snow on the surface, in meters 
• SWE—the amount of water stored in snow, in meters 
Each GRIB file with multiple parameters was split into an individual 
parameter GRIB file using the open source wgrib software utility available 
from NOAA (NWS 2005). Upon parameter separation, each separated 
parameter GRIB file was then converted into Network Common Data

data processing applications. The conversion was performed by the open-
source National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR) Command 
Language (NCL) software (NCAR-NCL, n.d.). NCL allows users to perform 
a variety of statistical and visual analyses of meteorological datasets. The 
ncl_convert2nc tool included within the package allows for conversion of 
GRIB files to netCDF (NCAR-NCL 2019).

2.2.3 Point Observations Using Meteorological Aerodrome Report (METAR) 
At the recommendation of project stakeholders, additional meteorological 
point observational data was obtained to provide limited verification of 
reanalysis data in key locations. These real-time observations are available 
in the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) standardized 
Meteorological Aerodrome Report (METAR) format (ICAO 2021). 
Typically, each station reports meteorological parameters at a minimum of 
hourly thresholds, often through automated means. Thirty years of 
METAR observations were obtained for stations within the area of 
interest. In some cases, certain stations were not available for the entire 
period because of budgetary and technological considerations of the 
individual countries providing the observations.

Once the raw METAR observations were obtained, an initial processing of

2.3 Geospatial Analyses 
The processes for geospatial analysis are described in detail by Barrett et 
al. (2025) SR-25, Development and Management of Arctic Zonal 
Classification Products. Below is a brief overview of the geospatial 
analyses performed. Section 4, “Geospatial Characterization Results”

2.3.1 Metric Calculations 
Four main metrics were calculated from the hourly ERA5 reanalysis data 
files across six different time scales to characterize prevailing conditions in 
the circumpolar north. The following time scales were used to calculate 
various statistics and metrics: 
• Hourly 
• Six-hour blocks (00:00–05:59 UTC, 06:00–11:59 UTC, 12:00–
17:59 UTC, 18:00–23:59 UTC) 
• Daily (00:00–23:59 UTC) 
• Weekly (7–8 days) 
• Biweekly (14–16 days) 
• Monthly 
• Seasonal (October–March) 
The average metric represents the mean value of each meteorological 
parameter within a specific time period across all 30 years of data 
(January 1991–October 2020). For six-hour time periods (e.g., 
3 December 00:00–05:59 UTC), the average was calculated using all 
hourly files within that period across the 30 year record (180 hourly files 
per six-hour period). For wind direction specifically, a circular mean was 
employed to account for the cyclical nature of directional data. 
@@@ PAGE 22 @@@
ERDC/CRREL TR-26-5 12 
 
The extreme metric captures the single most severe value recorded within 
each time period across the entire 30 year dataset for each grid cell. For 
temperature variables (SKT, 2T, WCT, STL1), this represents the minimum 
recorded value. For WSPD, SD, and SL, the maximum value was extracted. 
The frequency metric quantifies the percentage of single hours during 
which a variable exceeded a specified threshold over the course of the 
winter. The thresholds used are those specified in Arctic Doctrine Army 
Techniques Publications (ATP) 3-90.96, Table 1-3 and TM 4-33.31, Table 
2, as well as the Combat Weather Teams stoplight chart. For each grid 
cell, the total number of hours exceeding the threshold was divided by the 
total number of hours in the time period and multiplied by 100 to express 
the result as a percentage. 
The length of consecutive extreme events metric provides a detailed 
method for understanding how long intense environmental conditions 
persist. By analyzing thirty years of data, researchers track the duration of 
events that rise above a specific intensity threshold. For each day, the 
method identifies and measures separate periods when conditions exceed 
the threshold. First, the average duration of extreme events within each 
24 hr period is calculated using the raw hourly reanalysis data. Then, these 
daily measurements are averaged across the entire 30-year dataset. The 
analysis excluded six-hour time scales because of insufficient temporal 
resolution. For time scales larger than daily (weekly, biweekly, monthly, 
and seasonal), the daily consecutive values were averaged across the 
respective period. 
2.3.2 Generation of Static Maps 
A standardized mapping workflow was implemented to ensure consistency 
across all visualizations. Custom map layout templates and symbology files 
were created to maintain visual coherence across all maps for each 
parameter, metric, and time scale combination. The maps were then 
evaluated and quality controlled manually (Barrett et al. 2025). 
Further detailed information regarding the ERA5 reanalysis data variables 
and detailed process and methodology for map generation can be found in 
the US Army Engineer Research and Development Center (ERDC) Special 
Report, Development and Management of Arctic Zonal Characterization 
Products (Barrett et al. 2025).

3.2 Arctic Military Equipment and Gear for Specific Zones 
Operations in extreme Arctic environments require unique clothing and 
equipment solutions specific to each of the defined areas and variations in 
terrain. For example, military operations in the boreal forests of 
Scandinavia and elsewhere, rely heavily on skis and snowshoes and 
tracked vehicles to facilitate maneuver in deep snow conditions. Soldier 
clothing must be light-weight, well insulated, and suited to wet snow 
conditions. Camouflage patterns must be matched to snow covered forests 
with shades of white, green, and brown. Soldiers are able to operate using 
unheated shelters for short periods of time and overhead tree cover helps 
to mask Soldier movements and locations. 
Operations in the extreme cold of the Arctic tundra and on sea ice requires 
more heavily insulated clothing and camouflage patterns matched to the 
whites, blues, and grays of a snowy environment devoid of trees. 
Movement is largely restricted to snowmobiles and other tracked vehicles, 
and Soldiers will require heated shelters for all but the shortest durations. 
Soldiers that operate in cold environments, such as the 11th Airborne 
Division (ABN DIV) stationed in Alaska are issued specialized winter 
uniforms, allowing them to operate in extreme environments. Soldiers are 
issued a seven-layer clothing system called the Extended Cold Weather 
Clothing (ECWC) System. This system is adaptable and can be worn in 
multiple configurations to ensure proper insulation and decrease the 
likeliness of cold weather injuries. 
Level 1 is a lightweight, long sleeve top and full-length bottoms, 
constructed out of a silk-weight moisture-wicking polyester, they are 
commonly referred to as silks. Level 2 is a mid-weight insulation layer 
made of a polyester grid fleece design. Level 3 is a fleece cold weather 
jacket that can be used as either the primary insulative layer or an outer 
garment. Level 4 is a wind cold weather jacket made of a lightweight, wind 
resistant, and water-repellant material. This is a minimum outer shell 
layer. Level 5 is a soft-shell outer layer jacket and trousers. Level 6 is the 
extreme cold or wet weather jacket and trousers. These are waterproof and 
used in prolonged or hard rain, typically used when temperatures are 
alternating between freezing and thawing. Level 7 is the extreme cold 
weather parka and trousers. These are the warmest of the outer layers and

are used to provide superior warmth while still being packable and 
lightweight (TM 10-845-236-10; DA 2014).

It is important to recognize that these thresholds are general guidelines, 
and actual minimum tolerance levels can vary significantly between 
individuals. Each Soldier has a unique response to low temperatures from 
personal comfort, physiology, acclimation, and experience. Caution is 
advised when operating near the minimum effective limits of issued gear. 
It is always best to avoid excessive sweating and moisture buildup by 
maintaining a state of comfortably cool/warm—cool enough to prevent 
overheating, but warm enough to sustain performance and safety.

5 Summary 
The results of this project have produced a significant amount of 
information, data, and analyses in a relatively short time span. Over 
40,000 maps of environmental Arctic and Subarctic conditions have been 
generated, validated, and archived for ready use (see Deliverables in 
Section 6.2). The compilation of almost 30 years of hourly reanalysis data 
for the circumpolar Arctic is archived and easily transferable to future 
users of these data. Additionally, these data are all parsed into key 
temporal intervals for operational use. 
In reviewing the results of the generated maps for coincident extreme 
environmental parameters, we have concluded that while temperatures 
below −65°F or winds above 25 kn do occur, the frequency of such events 
is low (<10%). In terms of operational planning purposes, specific materiel 
that is operable down to temperatures not quite as low as −65°F can still 
be deployed and operated in many locations. Average snow load rarely 
exceeds the 25 lb/ft2 threshold, but can reach over 50 lb/ft2 in many areas 
especially by February and March. 
The comparisons between the ERA5 reanalysis data and the METAR 
observations show relatively good agreement and low standard 
deviation. There are a few anomalies where the ERA5 data and METAR 
air temperature observations deviate in a consistent matter, which is 
likely because of  varying heights of weather instrumentation collecting 
observational data. The observed METAR  wind speeds trend higher than 
the ERA5 wind speeds, which is likely because of  the ability of the 
observational recorded data to capture extremes better than the gridded 
reanalysis dataset. In -depth analysis and discussion of these 
observations is detailed in  the Special Report, Development and 
Management of Arctic Zonal Characterization Products: Geospatial 
Database (Barrett et al. 2025 ).

6 Discussion and Recommendations 
6.1 Considerations for Soldiers 
Cold environments are particularly difficult for soldiers because of the 
increased need for protective clothing and insulated shelters. Common 
atmospheric factors in cold environments include temperature, 
precipitation (snow and ice), fog, and wind. Low temperatures may also 
impair equipment operation, either temporarily or permanently, by 
changing the physical properties of materials. Hardening and brittleness 
of material, differential contraction of dissimilar materials, increased 
viscosity of lubricants, changes in electronic components, stiffening of 
shock mounts, and freezing of water are just a few of the potential 
problems. Ice accumulation on structures and equipment can cause 
damage and interfere with communication, visibility, and mobility. 
Aircraft such as helicopters and uncrewed aerial vehicles (UAVs) are 
especially vulnerable to icing, which can eliminate these key assets from 
operation. Accumulated snow also affects performance of electromagnetic 
sensors and additionally imposes a structural load on buildings, shelters, 
vehicles, and other equipment. There are human psychological and 
physiological challenges to operating in these extreme environments and 
the need for sustained training in these conditions cannot be overstated.  
6.2 Deliverables and Future Needs 
The deliverables of this project are ready for transition to a permanent 
location and use by the end-user community. To conclude this project in 
the 2025 fiscal year, the team at ERDC Cold Regions Research and 
Engineering Laboratory (CRREL) is working with the University of Alaska 
Fairbanks (UAF) Alaska Climate Research Center (ACRC) to transfer our 
compiled ERA5 dataset for hosting on their servers and long-term 
availability. UAF and CRREL are coproducing a landing page with 
description of the data on the ACRC’s website (https://akclimate.org/) to allow 
for easy downloading access for end-users. Additionally, all generated 
maps will be transferred and accessible via a separate tab on the same 
website. ERDC-CRREL is developing a user interface (UI) to allow end-
users to query their specified parameters and populate the corresponding 
map for download. For example, users will be able to select from the 
following options: 
@@@ PAGE 61 @@@
ERDC/CRREL TR-26-5 51 
 
• Region: The landmass from 60°N to 90°N, which includes the entire 
Arctic landmass plus portions of Subarctic, 0°–90°W, 90°W–180°E, 
180°E–90°E, and 90°E–0°. 
• Variable: SKT, 2T, SD, WSPD, WCT, STL1, SL. 
• Metric: average, extreme, frequency of extreme event (threshold 
specified), length of extreme event (threshold-specified). 
• Interval: six hours, daily, weekly, biweekly, monthly, October–March

• Month: October, November, December, January, February, and March. 
Once this UI is available, end-users simply need to specify their desired 
parameters and a map will be generated and ready for download. This will 
allow users to download multiple maps and examine the potential for 
coincident extreme conditions. 
In addition to this technology transfer effort, there are several options and 
potential pathways these datasets and maps can be integrated into end-
user-defined tools. Although these efforts do not fall within the scope of 
the current project, they should be explored in future efforts. A useful 
effort could be to develop a standalone UI that generates maps on-demand 
as opposed to pulling from a static archive. This could additionally include 
the ability to overlap maps that narrow in on specific parameters that are 
of interest to view jointly. While the products developed for this project are 
specific to the Arctic region, the ERA5 dataset includes global coverage 
that could be applied to other regions of the world with regional 
observations, or globally. Lastly, the ability to zoom in to a resolution 
higher than 31 km on the maps is past the limitations of the datasets used 
in this study, but could be of interest in a future effort to allow for 
narrowing into smaller regions and viewing conditions at a higher fidelity 
than regionally.

14. ABSTRACT 
The US military develops and updates environmental parameters specified for the sustainment of operations throughout the world. These 
requirements are generally based on environmental data providing a baseline of temperature, wind, and precipitation expectati ons for each 
location. Observational data for Arctic regions is limited because of the remote and sparsely occupied geographical conditions. To address 
the need for updating these requirements, a 30-year analysis of meteorological conditions was conducted using a European Centre for 
Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) global reanalysis dataset over the Arctic and Subarctic region, defined by latitude 60° –90° 
North for this project. Raw hourly datasets were acquired, and the minimum temperatures, maximum wind speeds, maximum snow 
depths, and averages were determined over the period 1991–2020 between the months of October and March for each parameter. These 
were then visualized with geospatial analysis, producing a variety of maps designed to assist with the classification of para meters in Arctic 
zones of operation across a range of temporal resolutions. Finally, a review of operational limits for military equipment was  conducted to 
match northern zones of operation with suitable capabilities dependent on environmental conditions .
